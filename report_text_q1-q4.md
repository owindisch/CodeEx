# Assignment 2 – Report text, questions 1–4 (group 18, Massachusetts)

## Question 1 – Data inspection and cleaning

The data set contains 1,620 census tracts in Massachusetts and 39 variables. Inspecting the summary statistics shows that several variables take the value −666,666,666, which cannot be a real estimate (e.g. a negative median income or median age). This value occurs 358 times, exclusively in variables that the variable description marks as "may contain special codes": median rent (124), median home value (65), median household income (35), the Gini index, median rooms and median year built (29 each), household size (27) and median age (20). No other special codes occur.

According to the Census Bureau, −666666666 is an annotation value meaning that "the estimate could not be computed because there were an insufficient number of sample observations" (U.S. Census Bureau, *Notes on ACS Estimate and Annotation Values*). For a median this happens when a tract has no or too few cases in the underlying distribution, for instance no renters for median rent. The value is therefore a label, not a measurement. Keeping it would dominate every mean, correlation and regression, and replacing it by zero or the mean would invent information that does not exist. We therefore recode all these values as missing (NA).

Next we checked the population of the tracts. Eighteen tracts have no inhabitants at all, and a further nine have between 3 and 202 inhabitants. These are almost all special land-use tracts (tract codes 98xx, e.g. airports, harbour islands and parks) or water-only tracts (codes 99xx), which the Census Bureau creates for areas with little or no population. For tracts without inhabitants the dependent variable cannot be computed. For the very small tracts it rests on only a handful of survey responses, which shows in the data: pct_wfh is exactly 0% or 100% in five of them. Such values reflect sampling noise rather than neighbourhood characteristics. The distribution of population shows a clear gap between 202 and 453 inhabitants, so we drop all tracts with fewer than 300 inhabitants, together with tracts for which pct_wfh is missing. We do not use the Census guideline of 1,200 inhabitants as the cut-off, because that would also remove 13 regular residential tracts with valid estimates.

After cleaning, **1,593 tracts remain** (27 removed). A small number of independent variables still contain missing values, mainly median rent (98 tracts) and median home value (39 tracts). We keep these tracts, because their other variables are valid. Deleting them would also bias the sample: median rent is mostly missing in owner-occupied suburbs (87% owner-occupied vs. 59% elsewhere). Missing values are instead excluded per analysis: pairwise for correlations, and listwise per regression model.

## Question 2 – Median home value and median household income

The histograms of median home value and median household income are both strongly right-skewed and show a striking spike in the highest bin. Eleven tracts have exactly the same maximum home value of 2,000,001 dollars, and 30 tracts have exactly the same maximum household income of 250,001 dollars. Since a median is computed from hundreds of households, identical values in so many tracts cannot be a coincidence.

The explanation is top-coding. The ACS computes medians from grouped distributions whose highest interval is open-ended. When a tract's median falls in that interval, the Census Bureau does not publish the value but only the lower bound with a plus sign, "2,000,000+" or "250,000+". In the data these appear as 2000001 and 250001 (U.S. Census Bureau, *Notes on ACS Estimate and Annotation Values*; *Notes on ACS Data*). For these tracts we therefore only know that the true median is at least the cap; it could be much higher. The same applies to median rent (22 tracts at "3,500+") and median year built (389 tracts at "1939 or earlier").

As predictors, these variables are censored. For the richest tracts the predictor is measured with error and stays constant, while pct_wfh keeps varying. These tracts have considerably more working from home: 32.8% for top-coded income tracts against 17.9% for the others. A linear model would place all these high values at a single point on the x-axis, which distorts the estimated slope at the top of the distribution and makes it impossible to say anything about tracts above the cap.

We decide to keep the top-coded tracts, because they are real and relevant neighbourhoods. They are exactly the high-income, high-WFH areas that matter for the company, and dropping them would make the sample selective. We keep the capped value and add a dummy variable that equals 1 for top-coded tracts. In a regression this dummy captures the average difference of the censored group, so the slope of the continuous variable is mainly estimated from the uncensored tracts. Because both variables are strongly right-skewed, we also use their logarithms. More complex alternatives, such as a censored (Tobit-type) approach or imputing values above the cap, are not worth the extra complexity, since only 1–2% of the tracts are affected.

## Question 3 – Exploratory analysis

The dependent variable pct_wfh ranges from 0% to 52.4% across tracts, with a median of 17.3% and a mean of 18.2%. Working from home is thus common, but the differences between neighbourhoods are large: the middle half of the tracts lies between 10.2% and 25.0%.

We plotted pct_wfh against a selection of independent variables (Figures X–Y; all variables in the appendix) and computed their correlations with pct_wfh. The strongest positive relations are with education and occupation: the share of adults with at least a bachelor's degree (r = 0.81) and the share of workers in management, business, science and arts occupations (r = 0.80). Both scatter plots show a clear, almost linear relation. Income and wealth are also strongly positively related: median household income (r = 0.66; 0.63 in logs), median home value (0.64) and median rent (0.54). The relation with income is convex: working from home increases slowly at low incomes and much faster at high incomes. Broadband access is moderately positive (r = 0.41) and also non-linear, with WFH mainly increasing when more than about 90% of households have broadband.

The strongest negative relations are with the share of adults whose highest degree is high school (r = −0.73), production, transportation and material-moving occupations (r = −0.64), service occupations (r = −0.60), construction and maintenance occupations (r = −0.52), and the share of households receiving SNAP (r = −0.55). These findings are consistent with the idea that working from home depends on whether jobs can be done remotely: office and knowledge work can, while service, production and construction work requires physical presence. Commuting variables, age structure and housing type are only weakly correlated with pct_wfh (|r| < 0.3).

Three caveats apply. First, correlation does not imply causation. Education, occupation, income and home value are strongly correlated with each other, so their separate effects cannot be identified from bivariate correlations, and multicollinearity will be an issue in the regression. Second, several relations are non-linear, which suggests using logs or other transformations. Third, the 5-year estimates pool responses from 2020 to 2024, when working from home first rose sharply and then partly declined, so a tract's value is an average over a changing period.

## Question 4 – Commuting shares and mechanical relations

In the ACS every worker reports one principal means of transportation to work, and "worked from home" is one of the answer categories. Driving alone, carpooling, public transport, walking and other modes therefore add up to 100% of all workers together with working from home. If the shares of people who drive alone or take public transport were computed as a share of all workers, they would be mechanically linked to the dependent variable: every additional percentage point of working from home would automatically reduce the commuting shares. A regression would then find a strong negative relation that reflects an accounting identity rather than a neighbourhood characteristic, and the model would partly be explaining pct_wfh with itself. Such a variable would also be useless for prediction, since it can only be computed once working from home is already known. Expressed as a share of commuters, the variables describe how those who do travel get to work, which is a genuine characteristic of the neighbourhood.

Our data illustrates this. The correlation between pct_wfh and the share driving alone is −0.19 when measured as a share of commuters. When we convert it to a share of all workers (multiplying by (100 − pct_wfh)/100), the correlation becomes −0.54, so most of the relation is then mechanical.

Other independent variables are also partly mechanically related to pct_wfh. The mean commuting time is, by definition, computed only for workers who did not work at home (U.S. Census Bureau, *ACS Subject Definitions*). If people with long commutes are the most likely to work from home, the average commute of the remaining commuters changes as a result of working from home rather than explaining it. The commuting shares themselves, even as shares of commuters, are affected by a similar composition effect: if office workers who used public transport switch to working from home, the transit share among the remaining commuters falls. The number of workers is the denominator of pct_wfh and should not be used as a predictor. Finally, variables such as broadband access and the share of households without a vehicle are not mechanically related, but they may partly be a consequence of working from home (reverse causality), which must be kept in mind when interpreting them.
