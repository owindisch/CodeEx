1. Introduction

Since acquiring a new customer costs more than retaining an existing one, and customers become more profitable the longer they stay (Gupta et al., 2006), it is in a company’s best interest to minimise churn, i.e. customers ending their contract and switching to a competitor. This report therefore investigates the churn behaviour of 4,000 customers of a telecom provider to gain insight into the factors that play a role in their decision to leave, addressing the question: which customer characteristics are related to churn, and which customers should a telecom provider target to reduce it?

2. Data

The data were provided by a telecom provider and contain 4,000 customers. For each customer, the data contain Churn (yes/no; 1,500 or 37.5% churned) and twelve predictors without missing values: the contract term (Contract; 59.7% month-to-month), months with the company (tenure; median 25.5), the monthly and total amount charged (MonthlyCharges, median $73; TotalCharges), whether the customer has a partner, phone service or multiple lines, the internet provider (InternetService: DSL, fibre optic or none) and four add-ons (online security, online backup, device protection, tech support). Churn differs strongly between groups: 56.0% of month-to-month customers churned against 4.5% of two-year customers, and 54.6% of fibre customers against 27.7% of DSL customers. The data are randomly split into a training set of 2,800 (70%) and a test set of 1,200 customers (30%), stratified by Churn so both sets have the same churn rate of 37.5%. Always predicting “no churn” thus gives a baseline accuracy of 62.5%.

3. Methods

To identify which characteristics are related to churn, a decision tree is used. Since churn is categorical, CART (Classification And Regression Trees; Breiman et al., 1984) builds a classification tree, which predicts the outcome through a series of yes/no questions, such as “is X₁ below 10?”. Each question splits the observations into two more homogeneous groups; the final groups (leaves) predict their most common class, giving easy-to-read if-then rules (Yildirim & Kübler, 2023). Formally, each observation has a response Y in one of K classes and p predictors X₁, …, X_p. The tree is built in five steps.

First, the tree is grown on a training set by recursive binary splitting (James et al., 2013; Tan et al., 2014). At each node m, every predictor X_j and cut-point s is tried. Each candidate splits the node into {X_j < s} and {X_j ≥ s}. The split with the lowest weighted Gini impurity of the two new nodes is chosen, where
G_m = 1 − Σ (k = 1, …, K) p̂_mk²
and p̂_mk is the share of training observations in node m that belong to class k. A pure node has G_m = 0. In a leaf, p̂_mk is the predicted probability of class k.

Second, because a fully grown tree also fits noise and this often leads to overfitting, it is pruned (Tan et al., 2014). In rpart (Therneau & Atkinson, 2023), the pruned tree is the subtree T that minimises
R_cp(T) = R(T) + cp ⋅ |T| ⋅ R(T₀),
where R(T) is the share of misclassified training observations, |T| the number of leaves of T, R(T₀) the misclassification rate of the tree without splits, and cp ≥ 0 the complexity parameter. A split is only kept if it lowers the relative error by at least cp, so a larger cp gives a smaller tree.

Third, cp is chosen by 10-fold cross-validation (James et al., 2013). The training set is divided into 10 parts and the tree is fitted on 9 parts. It is then tested on the remaining part, in turn. This gives a cross-validated error and its standard error; lower is better. The one-standard-error rule selects the smallest tree whose error is within one standard error of the minimum (Hastie et al., 2009).

Fourth, the selected tree is evaluated on a separate test set by its accuracy (share of correct predictions), precision P (share of predicted positives that are correct), recall R (share of actual positives detected) and F1 score F₁ = 2PR/(P+R) (Tan et al., 2014). Higher is better; accuracy should clearly exceed the baseline accuracy of always predicting the majority class. Finally, a predictor’s variable importance is its share of the total Gini reduction; higher is more important (de Oña et al., 2016).

4. Results

The full tree has 94 splits; the cross-validated error is lowest (0.622) at 15 splits. The one-standard-error rule selects cp = 0.0076, a tree with 8 splits and 9 leaves (Figure 1).

Contract type is the first split in the tree and also the most important variable (23.3% of the total importance), followed by tenure (17.6%) and total charges (12.5%; Appendix A, Table A1). Customers with a one- or two-year contract make up 40% of all customers, and only 10% of them leave. A longer contract makes switching harder, so these customers are fairly safe.

The riskiest group is new fibre-optic customers: customers on a month-to-month contract who have been with the company for less than 16 months. They make up 18% of all customers, and 81% of them leave. Fibre is the most expensive service, and these customers have not yet had time to become loyal. Fibre customers who stay longer are less likely to leave, unless their monthly bill is $95.55 or more; then 62% still churn. For month-to-month customers with DSL or no internet, the length of the relationship matters most. After six months, only 28% leave, but 63% of newer customers without online backup do. Customers who use more services thus seem more attached to the provider.

On the 1,200 test customers, the tree predicts churn correctly in 76.5% of cases. This is well above the 62.5% baseline and close to the 78.0% on the training data, so the tree does not overfit; the unpruned tree only reaches 74.0%. Of the customers the tree flags as churners, 70.1% actually leave (precision), and it finds 65.1% of all churners (recall), giving an F1 score of 0.68.

5. Conclusions and Discussion

Churn mainly depends on how strongly customers are tied to the provider: contract, tenure, internet service and price. The provider should target month-to-month customers in their first year, especially fibre customers (e.g. discounts on longer contracts), and promote extra services such as online backup to new DSL customers. The analysis could be improved: a lower cut-off would catch more of the 34.9% missed churners, and satisfaction or price data could reveal causes.
